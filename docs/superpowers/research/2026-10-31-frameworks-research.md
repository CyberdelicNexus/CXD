## fw-iceberg · Iceberg Model
- **Origin:** No documented single originator. Widely used in systems thinking and often associated with Peter Senge (The Fifth Discipline, 1990), Donella Meadows' teaching, and Daniel Kim's four-level form. The Waters Center for Systems Thinking publishes its own handout ("Iceberg: Seeing What's Below the Surface", 2020). Confidence: low on origin, high on content.
- **Canonical steps:** 1. Events: what happened, the visible symptom. 2. Patterns: what trends and recurrences show over time. 3. Structures: the policies, incentives, layouts, flows and relationships that produce the patterns. 4. Mental models: the beliefs and assumptions that keep the structures in place. Then ask where the leverage is (deeper levels give more).
- **Failure modes:** Stopping at events and fixing symptoms; treating the four layers as a strict ladder when real systems loop; blaming people instead of structures; it oversimplifies (an entry point, not a model of the system).
- **Licence / trademark:** Idea unowned. The Waters Center handout is CC BY-NC-ND (no adaptation, no commercial use), so do not copy or adapt its text or guiding questions; our wording must be original.
- **Adaptation notes for CXD:** Keep the four layer names, the standard vocabulary. Use experience-design prompts (the empty room, drop-off in hour two, a crammed schedule, the assumption that guests want more content). Origin line and sourceUrl per correction 1.
- **Sources:** https://www.thinknpc.org/resource-hub/the-iceberg-model/ ; https://sketchplanations.com/the-iceberg-model ; https://toolbox.nesslabs.com/iceberg-model ; https://rhntc.org/sites/default/files/resources/supplemental/Iceberg-seeing_whats_below_the_surface_handout.pdf (Waters Center handout, licence from search snippet, PDF text not readable) ; https://thesystemsthinker.com/ (read: does not mention the iceberg)

## fw-causal-loop · Causal Loop Map
- **Origin:** System dynamics was created by Jay Forrester at MIT in the mid-1950s (Industrial Dynamics, 1961, uses flow diagrams). Causal loop diagrams with polarity notation grew from that community and were popularised for managers by Peter Senge's The Fifth Discipline (1990). Confidence: medium.
- **Canonical steps (Lannon, The Systems Thinker):** 1. Name the variables (things that can change over time). 2. Draw links with arrows, labelled same direction (s or +) or opposite direction (o or -). 3. Label each loop reinforcing (R) or balancing (B): count the opposite links; an even count (including zero) is reinforcing, an odd count is balancing. 4. Talk through the loop as a story to check it matches the behaviour you are describing.
- **Failure modes:** Verbs instead of nouns for variables (put the action on the arrow); leaving out significant delays; diagramming only the problem or building a mega-diagram; reading one arrow instead of tracing the whole loop.
- **Licence / trademark:** Method unowned. The Systems Thinker article is copyright Pegasus Communications; paraphrase, do not copy.
- **Adaptation notes for CXD:** Use canvas edges for links. Prefer plus and minus (or "same" and "opposite") over s and o; state the sign rule in our own words. Optional delay mark. Origin per correction 2; cite the Systems Thinker page for steps only.
- **Sources:** https://thesystemsthinker.com/causal-loop-construction-the-basics/ (read) ; https://thesystemsthinker.com/?p=4823 (via search) ; https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/System_Dynamics (via search) ; https://fasterthannormal.co/decision-tools/causal-loop-diagrams (via search)

## fw-theory-of-change · Theory of Change
- **Origin:** Carol Weiss popularised the term in work linked to the Aspen Institute Roundtable on Community Change, leading to New Approaches to Evaluating Comprehensive Community Initiatives (1995). Roots of theory-based evaluation go back to the late 1950s. The Roundtable and later ActKnowledge (theoryofchange.org) promoted backward mapping. Confidence: high.
- **Canonical steps (theoryofchange.org):** 1. Identify the long-term goal. 2. Work back from it to the outcomes (conditions) that must be in place and how they relate causally (backward mapping). 3. Map them in an outcomes framework. 4. Identify the activities or interventions that will produce those outcomes. The site also covers assumptions and indicators.
- **Failure modes:** Treating it as a linear inputs-to-outputs chain; leaving assumptions unexamined or vague; letting the diagram become a fixed plan or a form to fill in; fetishising the diagram over the reflection.
- **Licence / trademark:** Concept free to use. theoryofchange.org content is copyright The Center for Theory of Change, Inc., no open licence; use our own wording and link.
- **Adaptation notes for CXD:** Map to CXD's state-to-trait vocabulary (lasting trait at the end, states between, activities and inputs at the start, drawn backward). Include an explicit assumptions lane to counter the main criticism. Origin per correction 5.
- **Sources:** https://www.theoryofchange.org/what-is-theory-of-change/ (read) ; https://www.theoryofchange.org/what-is-theory-of-change/toc-background/toc-origins/ (read) ; https://www.thinknpc.org/blog/theory-of-change-criticisms/ (via search) ; https://www.nesta.org.uk/blog/whats-wrong-with-theories-of-change/ (via search)

## fw-hmw-crazy8 · How Might We + Crazy 8s
- **Origin:** How Might We: coined by Min Basadur at Procter & Gamble in the 1970s (exposure to creative problem solving in 1971 via Sid Parnes and Alex Osborn's institute); popularised by IDEO and the d.school; Berger's 2012 HBR article "The Secret Phrase Top Innovators Use" is the usual reference. Crazy 8s: speed-sketching exercise from the GV design sprint (Jake Knapp, Sprint, 2016; the sprint was created at Google in 2010 and brought to GV in 2012). Confidence: high for Basadur and Knapp, medium for the IDEO chain.
- **Canonical steps:** HMW: turn problems and insights into open questions beginning "How might we", then cluster and pick. Crazy 8s: fold a sheet into eight frames, sketch one idea per frame, one minute each (eight minutes in the book; an early GV article says five), no polish, then share and vote.
- **Failure modes:** HMW too broad ("help people be healthier") gives no starting point; too narrow smuggles in the solution ("build an app that..."); skipping the reframing; polishing instead of getting past the obvious first ideas.
- **Licence / trademark:** Both are methods, unowned. "Design Sprint" is associated with GV and Google; do not use it as a product name. Sprint book text is copyrighted; paraphrase only.
- **Adaptation notes for CXD:** Calibration test for HMW: could ten very different experiences answer it? Use eight frames on the canvas. Say "sprint-style" and credit GV. sourceUrl per correction 8.
- **Sources:** https://www.basadur.com/?p=7453 (read) ; https://www.gv.com/sprint/ (read) ; https://designsprintkit.withgoogle.com/methodology/phase3-sketch/crazy-8s (via search) ; https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-might-we-questions/ (via search) ; https://designkit.org/methods/3.html (via search)

## fw-scamper · SCAMPER
- **Origin:** Bob Eberle formalised the mnemonic in SCAMPER: Games for Imagination Development (1971), building on Alex Osborn's idea-spurring question lists in Applied Imagination (1953). Confidence: medium (secondary sources; book not read).
- **Canonical steps:** Apply seven lenses to an existing thing: Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify (Magnify or Minify), Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse (or Rearrange). Generate ideas under each, then filter separately.
- **Failure modes:** Treating it as a guarantee of creativity (results depend on the user); using it to evaluate when it only generates; skipping facilitation; stopping after one round.
- **Licence / trademark:** Acronym and technique unowned.
- **Adaptation notes for CXD:** Our own examples per lens from installations and retreats; keep the standard seven words, since they are the mnemonic. Origin per correction 9.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SCAMPER (via search) ; https://imd.org/blog/innovation/scamper-method-design-thinking/ (via search) ; https://fasterthannormal.co/decision-tools/scamper (via search) ; https://www.6sigma.us/lean-tools/scamper-technique/ (via search)

## fw-impact-effort · Impact–Effort Matrix
- **Origin:** No documented originator. Common prioritisation 2x2; sources attribute it to lean/Six Sigma practice or MindTools' Action Priority Matrix. The Eisenhower matrix (urgency by importance) is a different tool popularised by Covey; no source documents a descent. Confidence: low on origin.
- **Canonical steps:** 1. List the ideas. 2. Score each for impact and effort. 3. Place on a 2x2: Quick wins (high impact, low effort), Major projects (high, high), Fill-ins (low, low), and the low-impact high-effort corner (Thankless tasks, Time sinks or Money pits). 4. Do quick wins first, plan major projects, drop the last quadrant.
- **Failure modes:** Estimation bias (effort is routinely underestimated); chasing quick wins and neglecting major work; scoring alone rather than as a group; treating placement as fixed instead of re-scoring.
- **Licence / trademark:** Unowned.
- **Adaptation notes for CXD:** Own quadrant names are fine (they are not standardised). Say "common practice" and drop the Eisenhower lineage claim (correction 10).
- **Sources:** https://untools.co/impact-effort-matrix/ (via search) ; https://thedigitalprojectmanager.com/productivity/impact-effort-matrix/ (via search) ; https://fasterthannormal.co/decision-tools/impact-effort-matrix (via search) ; https://vibe.us/blog/impact-vs-effort-matrix/ (via search)

## fw-premortem · Pre-mortem
- **Origin:** Gary Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem", Harvard Business Review, September 2007 (85(9): 18-19). It formalises the older concept of prospective hindsight (Mitchell, Russo and Pennington, 1989). Confidence: high.
- **Canonical steps (as Klein describes it):** The team assumes the project has just failed, each member independently writes plausible reasons, the reasons are collected, then the team assesses them and builds preventive actions into the plan. The aim is to make it safe for knowledgeable dissenters to speak.
- **Failure modes:** Running it after the plan is locked; letting senior voices dominate (write independently first); listing risks and never converting them into safeguards; the premise may be wrong, so surfaced threats may not be real. The 30% figure belongs to the 1989 study and may measure number of reasons, not accuracy (correction 12).
- **Licence / trademark:** Unowned method; the HBR article is copyrighted, paraphrase only.
- **Adaptation notes for CXD:** Frame as "the launch night failed". Convert causes into safeguards on the canvas. Use the corrected 30% wording or leave the statistic out.
- **Sources:** https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem (read, teaser only) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-mortem (read) ; https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/hbr-guide-to/9781422187319/14-chap5.xhtml (HBR Guide excerpt, via search snippet; direct fetch 403) ; Mitchell, Russo and Pennington (1989), Journal of Behavioral Decision Making 2(1): 25-38 (not read)

## fw-workback · Work-back Plan
- **Origin:** Generic project-management practice (backward scheduling from a fixed date, critical path method); no single originator. Unconfirmed; drafts keep "common practice" and should credit no one.
- **Canonical steps:** 1. Fix the live date. 2. List milestones and deliverables working backward from it. 3. Estimate each task's duration and dependencies. 4. Add buffer. 5. Assign owners. 6. Check feasibility: if the chain reaches a date before today, change scope or date.
- **Failure modes:** Assuming parallel work that is really sequential; ignoring external lead times (approvals, vendors, permits); zero buffer; overloading the same people across tasks.
- **Licence / trademark:** Unowned.
- **Adaptation notes for CXD:** Event plans have a fixed date, so protect the critical-path items (venue, permits, build) with buffer. Buffer sizes in sources (about 10-20% for event deadlines, 20-30% for critical-path tasks) are rules of thumb; say "add buffer" and do not state a number as a rule.
- **Sources:** https://traindems.org/resources/backwards-planning-template (via search) ; https://support.oculai.de/en/articles/626069-how-the-planning-engine-works (via search) ; https://ones.com/blog/?p=1109445 (via search)

## fw-value-prop · Value Proposition Canvas
- **Origin:** Alexander Osterwalder and Strategyzer; presented in Value Proposition Design (Osterwalder, Pigneur, Bernarda, Smith, 2014; date secondary-sourced). Confidence: high on authorship, medium on date.
- **Canonical steps:** Customer profile: jobs (functional, social, emotional), pains, gains. Value map: products and services, pain relievers, gain creators. Then check fit: connect pain relievers to pains and gain creators to gains; uncovered items are opportunity gaps. Work one customer segment at a time and start from the customer side.
- **Failure modes (Strategyzer):** Mixing several segments on one canvas; blurring jobs, pains and gains, or pains and pain relievers; listing only what your offer solves; assuming pains and gains instead of observing them; functional jobs only.

Adaptations and inclusion in software or for sale need Strategyzer AG's express permission; credit to Strategyzer.com and Strategyzer AG is required. See correction 14.
- **Adaptation notes for CXD:** Use our own title (for example "Audience Fit Map"), own wording and layout, only the generic customer-versus-offer fit idea, and a visible credit and link. Do not ship this one as "Value Proposition Canvas" without Strategyzer's permission. Amend the spec line "CC BY-SA (Strategyzer)".

## fw-aarrr · Pirate Metrics Funnel
- **Origin:** Dave McClure, "Startup Metrics for Pirates: AARRR!", Ignite Seattle, 2007 (one interview says 2006; the SlideShare page gives no year). Confidence: high on author and stages, medium on year.
- **Canonical steps (McClure's order):** 1. Acquisition: users come from various channels. 2. Activation: users have a happy first visit. 3. Retention: users come back. 4. Referral: users like it enough to refer others. 5. Revenue: users carry out some monetisation behaviour. Retention comes before Referral.
- **Failure modes:** Tracking vanity metrics (pageviews, likes, downloads) instead of outcome-linked numbers; aggregate numbers that hide cohort behaviour; applying a product-led funnel to brand or event work without adapting; critics (RARRA) argue retention should come first for weak-retention products.
- **Licence / trademark:** Acronym and method unowned; no trademark found. Deck copyright McClure.
- **Adaptation notes for CXD:** One lane per stage with a tactic and one metric. For live experiences reframe the stages (acquisition = ticket sales, activation = arrival and first hour, retention = return visits, referral = word of mouth, revenue = ticket and add-on spend). Keep McClure's order and note the software-startup origin.

## fw-ecosystem-map · Partner & Channel Ecosystem Map
- **Origin:** The power-interest idea is attributed to Aubrey L. Mendelow (1991), "Stakeholder Mapping", Proceedings of the 2nd International Conference on Information Systems, Cambridge MA (secondary citation, often via Scholes 1998 or Botten 2006). Partner ecosystem mapping is generic business practice with no originator. Confidence: medium on Mendelow; original not read.
- **Canonical steps:** Mendelow-style: 1. List stakeholders. 2. Rate each on power and interest. 3. Place them on a 2x2 and choose a strategy per quadrant (quadrant names not confirmed on a page I read). Ecosystem-style: 1. List the actors (platform owners and gatekeepers, complementors, enablers, distributors, end users). 2. Plot influence against dependence or reach. 3. Start with about 10-20 critical actors. 4. Review at least quarterly.
